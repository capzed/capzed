Trading costs · Educational guide

Crypto exchange fees, explained.

A low advertised trading fee does not always mean a cheap trade. Learn how maker and taker rates, spreads, slippage, futures funding and withdrawal charges combine to affect your actual cost.

AUTOMATET Editorial · Published 25 November 2026 · Educational content, not investment advice
The short version

Compare the total cost of the transaction—not a single fee percentage. The amount you pay or receive can be affected by the exchange's fee tier, order type, bid–ask spread, available liquidity, price movement, funding payments and withdrawal method.

1. Maker and taker fees

Many centralized exchanges use a maker–taker fee schedule. A maker order adds liquidity to the order book when it rests there before execution. A taker order removes available liquidity by matching against an existing order. The exact classification depends on how the order executes, not simply on whether you selected a limit or market order.

  • Maker: an order that rests on the book and is later matched, subject to the platform's rules.
  • Taker: an order that executes against orders already available in the book.
  • Marketable limit: a limit order that crosses the spread can execute immediately and incur taker fees for the matched quantity.

Exchanges may publish different rates for spot, margin, perpetual futures and delivery futures. Fee tiers may depend on rolling trading volume, account level, token holdings or other conditions. Always check the official fee schedule for the exact market and product.

2. The bid–ask spread

The bid is the highest displayed buying price; the ask is the lowest displayed selling price. The difference is the spread. If you buy at the ask and immediately sell at the bid, the spread can create a cost even before trading fees are counted.

Spread = Best ask − Best bid

A narrow spread can indicate a more competitive market at that moment, but it does not tell the whole story. Check order-book depth at the size you intend to trade. A market with a tight top-of-book spread can still have limited liquidity for a larger order.

3. Slippage and market impact

Slippage is the difference between the expected execution price and the actual average execution price. It can happen when prices move while an order is being processed or when the order consumes several levels of available liquidity.

For a market order, the average fill may be worse than the best price visible before submission. A large order can move through the order book; on an automated market maker, the size of the trade relative to pool liquidity can change the quoted price.

Before placing a large order, inspect depth or the swap quote at the intended size. A fee comparison using only the smallest displayed rate can be misleading.

4. Futures funding is not the same as a trading fee

Perpetual futures contracts commonly use periodic funding payments to help keep the contract price aligned with the underlying market. Depending on the funding rate and position direction, a trader may pay or receive funding. The rate can change, and payment timing and calculation rules differ by venue.

Funding is separate from the maker or taker fee charged when opening or closing a position. For a position held over several funding intervals, include possible funding payments in your cost estimate. Do not assume a displayed current rate will remain unchanged.

5. Deposits, withdrawals and conversion costs

Funding an account or moving assets off an exchange can add costs that are not visible in the trading fee table.

  • Deposit charges may depend on payment method, currency or third-party processor.
  • Crypto withdrawals may include a network fee or a platform-set withdrawal charge.
  • Fiat withdrawals can involve bank, intermediary or conversion charges.
  • Converting between currencies or stablecoins may involve a spread or separate conversion fee.

Check the selected network and asset carefully. The cheapest withdrawal route is not necessarily compatible with the receiving wallet or service.

6. A simple way to compare total trading cost

For a basic spot trade, use this checklist rather than assuming one universal formula applies to every exchange:

Estimated total cost = trading fee + spread cost + slippage/market impact + conversion or transfer costs

For derivatives, separately account for any funding paid or received during the holding period, as well as contract-specific costs and liquidation rules. The final result depends on order size, direction, market conditions, fee tier and execution method.

7. Example: why the lowest headline fee may not win

Imagine two venues quote different maker/taker rates. Venue A advertises a lower taker fee, but at the time you trade it has a wider spread and less depth at your order size. Venue B has a slightly higher taker fee but a tighter spread and deeper order book. Venue B could produce a better average execution price, even before other costs are considered.

This is an illustrative example, not a statement about any specific exchange. Fees and liquidity change over time; compare the same asset, product, order size and market conditions wherever possible.

8. Checklist before choosing an exchange

  • Confirm that the exchange and product are available in your jurisdiction.
  • Open the official fee schedule; verify spot versus futures and maker versus taker rates.
  • Check whether a discount requires a token balance, subscription or volume threshold.
  • Compare the live spread and order-book depth for the exact trading pair.
  • Estimate slippage using your intended order size and order type.
  • For perpetuals, review funding intervals, funding history and liquidation mechanics.
  • Check deposit, withdrawal, conversion and network charges.
  • Review custody, account-security, withdrawal and counterparty risks.

Frequently asked questions

Is a limit order always a maker order?

No. A limit order that executes immediately against resting orders can be treated as taker for the executed quantity. Check the exchange's order and fee rules.

Are maker fees always lower than taker fees?

Not always. Many exchanges set different maker and taker rates, but the relationship and any rebates depend on the platform, market and fee tier.

Does a zero trading fee mean a free trade?

Not necessarily. Spread, slippage, conversion, funding, network or withdrawal costs may still apply. Read the conditions attached to any zero-fee promotion.

Where can I find the correct fee rate?

Use the exchange's official fee schedule while logged in if your account tier affects the rate. Confirm the product, pair, region and any discount conditions.
